Land Settlement Reports & Historical Revenue Administration

Historical Settlement Reports were comprehensive administrative surveys executed by the British colonial administration in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries across the Central Provinces. Undertaken to demarcate village boundaries, assess agrarian productivity, regularize land revenue, and codify local proprietary titles, these surveys form foundational documentation for the economic and institutional history of eastern Vidarbha.

In the Wainganga river basin—encompassing modern Bhandara, Gondia, Chandrapur, and Gadchiroli districts—settlement officers systematically chronicled the agrarian pre-eminence of the Kohli (also rendered historically as Kohri or Kaulees) community. The settlement surveys documented their hereditary roles as village Patils, Malguzari proprietary landholders, master builders of earthen irrigation tanks (Malguzari Bandharas), and pioneering cultivators of superior sugarcane and transplanted rice.

Historical Framework & Revenue Administration

Prior to colonial intervention, land tenure under the Gond kingdoms and the subsequent Maratha administration (Bhonsles of Nagpur) operated primarily on customary revenue leases granted to village headmen (Malguzars or Patils). When the Central Provinces were formed in 1861, colonial authorities instituted regular settlement operations to replace temporary farming leases with formal, proprietary settlements (often thirty-year tenures).

Administrative Context & Analytical Caveat: Settlement reports were designed as fiscal instruments to fix colonial tax liabilities and categorize agrarian tenancies. Their sociological classifications reflected colonial administrative paradigms of their era; they are analyzed here as verified historical archives rather than modern statutory definitions.

Chanda District Settlements (Capt. Lucie Smith & Napier)

The initial comprehensive land settlement of Chanda (modern Chandrapur and Gadchiroli) was executed between 1867 and 1869 by Captain C. B. Lucie Smith, Deputy Commissioner and Settlement Officer. Captain Lucie Smith documented the profound impact of the Kohli community in the Brahmapuri and Garhchiroli parganas:

Official Commendation of Kohli Engineering & Cultivation

“The Kohris are the most industrious and intelligent cultivators in the district; they understand irrigation thoroughly and have constructed almost all the fine tanks which exist in the Garchiroli and Bramhapuri parganas... they are men of enterprise and substantial means.”

— Captain C. B. Lucie Smith, Report on the Land Revenue Settlement of the Chanda District, Central Provinces (1869)

Subsequent settlement revisions undertaken by A. B. Napier, ICS, reaffirmed the community's agricultural leadership. Napier observed that the Kohli cultivators maintained sophisticated communal management over tank sluices and water distribution, ensuring that during seasons of precarious rainfall, paddy crops under Kohli tanks survived unblemished.

Bhandara District Surveys (Lawrence & Hemingway)

The first regular thirty-year settlement of Bhandara District was concluded in 1867 by A. J. Lawrence. In his settlement report, Lawrence examined the expansive tract known as the Lake District of Vidarbha, comprising the tehsils of Sakoli and Bhandara:

  • Navegaon Bandh Proprietary Settlement: The iconic 24-mile perimeter reservoir was formally recorded under the Malguzari proprietary titles of the Shiwankar Kohli family, whose lineage had supervised the lake's hydraulic maintenance since the early 18th century.
  • Cadastral Demarcation: The surveys mapped thousands of village water bodies (talaos, bodis, and bandhi fields), establishing precise water shares allocated from community-built masonry outlets.
  • Hemingway Revision (1900): F. R. R. Hemingway corroborated that the Kohli Malguzars maintained superior tank conditions compared to other proprietary tracts, preventing famine in Sakoli during regional droughts.

Malguzari Administration & Hereditary Patil Roles

Under the Central Provinces Land Revenue Acts, the Malguzar occupied a dual position: as proprietary holder of village lands responsible for the state revenue demand (Jamma), and as civic manager of communal resources. Within Kohli villages, the office of Malguzar was almost invariably conjoined with the traditional office of Patil (village chief).

Village Civic & Judicial Authority

The Kohli Patil presided over customary village councils (Panchayats), arbitrated disputes over agricultural boundaries and field channels, and hosted visiting revenue officials. Their family residences (Gadhi or Wada) served as centers of village governance.

Public Works & Labour Mobilization

Malguzars directed collective desilting operations (Gala Upasane) before monsoon onset, maintained stone sluice gates (Thana), and mobilized customary cooperative labour to reinforce earthen embankments.

Agrarian Structure, Sugarcane & Paddy Cultivation

Settlement reports uniformly emphasized that Kohli agriculture was distinguished by its extraordinary capital investment, soil preparation, and technical complexity. Two agricultural staples defined their agrarian legacy:

Crop / Enterprise Settlement Observations Economic Significance
Sugarcane (Kathani variety) Described as the exclusive specialty of Kohli cultivators. Required 12–14 months of constant perennial irrigation from tanks via feeder canals. Produced highly prized jaggery (gur) processed in local boiling vats (Gurhale), traded extensively across central India.
Transplanted Paddy (Ropa) Pioneered advanced nursery transplantation (Aavni) in precisely leveled embanked terraces (Bandhis). Cultivated fragrant fine varieties including Chinnor and Luchai, yielding record bushels per acre.

Water Rights, Sluice Outlets & Feeder Channels

Settlement officers devoted detailed chapters to the hydraulic mechanics of Kohli tank systems. Unlike northern canal networks constructed by public works departments, these tank systems were entirely indigenous creations engineered with remarkable topographic understanding.

The surveys documented stone-embedded discharge flumes known locally as Khind or Thana, fitted with wooden plugs (Ghan) that regulated discharge according to crop growth phases. Land records noted that water was conducted via gravity-flow contour channels (Paat) across several adjoining villages, demonstrating inter-village coordination governed by mutual custom.

Settlement Demographics: The 19,739 Population Record

Village Records: Wajib-ul-arz & Nistar Rights

A critical legal component of colonial settlement operations was the preparation of the Wajib-ul-arz (Village Administration Paper). This document codified customary rights, obligations, and privileges governing village lands:

  • Irrigation Precedence: Documented the chronological order in which cultivators drew water from village tanks, prioritizing perennial crops and protecting tail-end fields.
  • Pani Patai Dues: Formalized customary water charges paid by downstream cultivators to the tank builder or Malguzar for structural upkeep.
  • Nistar Forest Privileges: Confirmed access to grazing lands (Gairan) and timber required for making ploughs, carts, and wooden sluice gates.

Orthographic Variations in Colonial Records

In British administrative documents and settlement rolls, phonetic transliteration of Indian community names into English resulted in varied orthographic forms. Researchers consulting primary archival registers should note that these variations identify the same agrarian community:

Kohli Kohri Kohiree Kohulee Koholee Kohoree Kaulees
