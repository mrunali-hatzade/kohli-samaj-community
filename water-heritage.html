Water Conservation Heritage

Traditional Water Management

Across the undulating plains of the historic Bhandara, Gondia, and Chanda districts, rural communities developed sophisticated water-harvesting systems adapted to the seasonal monsoon. Earthen embankments, stone spillways, and gravity-fed distribution channels sustained agriculture, supported village livelihoods, and created an enduring legacy of regional water stewardship.

Historical Interpretation Note

Historical records and community literature document the creation and maintenance of irrigation tanks within the administrative and agrarian frameworks of the Central Provinces. Individual tanks have distinct origins and diverse builders across communities. Statements regarding specific builders or community connections on this portal are grounded in identified colonial records (Settlement Reports, District Gazetteers) or explicitly noted as recorded regional traditions.

Malguzari Tanks

Agrarian Water Systems

Extensive networks of traditional irrigation tanks existed across the historic Central Provinces, particularly in the undulating tracts of the Wainganga river basin comprising Bhandara, Gondia, and Chanda districts. Developed by rural proprietors and cultivators to impound seasonal runoff from hill slopes and natural depressions, these water bodies provided crucial gravity-fed irrigation for rabi crops and protected staple kharif paddy during monsoon breaks.

Historical Terminology: In British administrative settlements of the 19th century, proprietary rights in village estates were recognized under the Malguzari system. Tanks situated within these estates were designated 'Malguzari tanks' with customary water rights and maintenance obligations recorded in village administration papers (Wajib-ul-arz).

Kohli Water-Management Tradition

Community Heritage

Historical revenue surveys and ethnographic accounts of the Central Provinces consistently highlight the Kohli community for their agrarian enterprise, irrigation skill, and systematic construction of water-storage works. British administrators observed their expertise in siting bunds across natural contours to create productive irrigated agricultural tracts in eastern Vidarbha.

Historical Archival Record

Major C. B. Lucie Smith, in the 1869 Land Revenue Settlement Report of Chanda District (para 138, p. 50), observed that the Kohlis were expert cultivators of sugarcane and constructed large irrigation reservoirs where ground conditions permitted. He noted: 'A Kohli will never take a village which has not a tank or the site for one.' This reputation for water engineering was later corroborated by R. V. Russell in the Bhandara District Gazetteer (1908, pp. 195–196) and 'The Tribes and Castes of the Central Provinces' (1916, Vol. III, pp. 493–497).

Source: Major C. B. Lucie Smith (Chanda Settlement Report, 1869, para 138) & R. V. Russell (1908 / 1916)

Traditional Tank Engineering

Historical water storage across the region relied on time-tested principles of topography, soil compaction, stone reinforcement and gravity-flow irrigation. Traditional terminology for these structures is documented across regional settlement reports and district gazetteers.

Earthen Embankments (Paal)

Earthen dam wall (paal) constructed across natural valley depressions using compacted clay and soil to impound monsoon runoff.

Stone Pitching & Facing

Dry stone pitching placed along the water-facing embankment slope to prevent wave erosion and reinforce structural stability.

Spillways (Saandwa)

The saandwa (waste weir or spillway) carved at a secure rocky flank to safely discharge excess floodwaters during heavy rainfall without overtopping the main bund.

Canals & Distributaries (Paat)

Network of field channels (paat or kawa) laid out along gentle natural contours to deliver gravity-flow irrigation to agricultural plots.

Sluice & Outlets (Dhoda / Mori)

Sub-surface stone masonry sluice (mori or dhoda) fitted with plugs or control gates through the embankment base to regulate seasonal water release.

Field Irrigation Distribution

Regulated gravity-flow distribution arranged according to customary village water-sharing agreements among command-area farmers.

Water and Agriculture

Reliable water storage enabled cultivation of water-dependent crops such as rice and sugarcane. Historical material prominently connects Kohli agricultural practice with sugarcane cultivation and jaggery production.

🌾
Rice (Paddy)

Major staple kharif crop of Vidarbha, heavily reliant on timely tank irrigation during seasonal rainfall dry spells.

🌱
Sugarcane

Historically grown with intensive tank irrigation in Kohli settlements, as prominently documented in 19th-century revenue surveys.

🍯
Jaggery Production

Traditional boiling and processing of sugarcane juice into high-quality gur, fostering an active rural cottage trade.

Regional Water Heritage

Navegaon Bandh

Prominent Water Landmark

Navegaon Bandh is one of the largest and most renowned historic reservoirs in eastern Vidarbha, situated in the southern hilly tract of Gondia district. The Central Provinces District Gazetteers: Bhandara District (1908, edited by R. V. Russell, pp. 195–196) records the local tradition that the reservoir 'is said to have been built by Kolu Patel Kohli' approximately three centuries ago. While this attribution is firmly preserved in regional folklore and community memory, no contemporary 18th-century Gond royal sanad or construction contract has survived in British or provincial archives to independently prove the legal terms of its origin. It remains an enduring symbol of community water stewardship and regional agrarian heritage.

Historical Record Attribution: Central Provinces District Gazetteers: Bhandara District (1908), pp. 195–196. Cautiously documented as a recorded regional tradition rather than an independently verified contractual grant.

Water as a Community Responsibility

Traditional village water systems depended not only on engineering design but equally on collective maintenance, periodic desiltation, customary water-sharing protocols, and seasonal bund repairs. Community cooperation and headmanship supervision were essential for keeping village irrigation systems functional.

Water and Village Life

Village tanks supported multiple interconnected dimensions of rural society, economy and ecology across eastern Vidarbha:

Agriculture

Essential irrigation for staple paddy crops and sugarcane plots during dry spells.

Livestock

Year-round drinking and bathing water for cattle, buffaloes and agricultural livestock.

Household Needs

Non-potable domestic water requirements, washing, and traditional village routines.

Village Ecology

Sustaining local groundwater levels, regional wells, aquatic flora, and bird habitats.

Knowledge Passed Through Generations

Much of the traditional knowledge surrounding catchment behaviour, water flow, soil characteristics, bund alignment, and seasonal water budgeting was practical expertise transmitted through hands-on experience across generations.

Historical sources and community traditions provide complementary perspectives on this heritage, with documentary archival evidence and living community memory clearly distinguished.

From Heritage to Conservation

Traditional water systems remain relevant to contemporary discussions about water conservation, groundwater recharge, agricultural resilience and restoration of village water bodies.
