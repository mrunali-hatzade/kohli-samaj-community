The Cultural Heritage of the Kohli Community (Marathi: कोहळी समाज संस्कृती) encompasses centuries of living social practices, indigenous water management ethics, agricultural customs, multi-generational family structures, and vibrant folk performing arts across eastern Maharashtra and Central India. Historical settlements spanning 518 villages in Bhandara, Gondia, Chandrapur, Gadchiroli, and Balaghat districts developed a harmonious rural culture deeply intertwined with seasonal agricultural rhythms, community tank ecosystems, and reciprocal village relationships. [1]

Unlike transient modern fashions, traditional Kohli culture was anchored in the Malguzari landholding framework, reciprocal support for village craftspeople (Bara Balutedar), multi-day matrimonial ceremonies, and community-wide stage theatre (Dandar). Community memory is preserved through oral testimonies, elder memoirs, and documented social customs.

1. Marriage Traditions & Rites [edit]

In Kohli community wedding traditions, ancient ceremonial practices and customary rites inherited through community cultural history continue to be observed, including the veneration of the village guardian mother deity (Matama Pujan), the turmeric smearing ceremony (Halad), and the customary reciprocal family gifting rite (Far takane) on the first day of wedding celebrations (Source Line 39). [1]

Community Oral Memoir: Traditional Wedding Customs from 60 Years Ago

“The marriage traditions of the Kohli community: the mandap canopy (mandodhari) supported by posts of salai and mowai trees, with the wedding mandap roofed with fresh green branches of the jamun tree. Bringing water from the river in damne bandi (water-barrel carts); a caravan of 40–50 racing carts (rengya) forming the groom's procession...”

— Er. Shalikram Borkar, Editor 'Reshimgathi', 'Kohli Samajachi Sanskruti — Part 3' (Personal oral community memoir; age 77, fourth-generation recollection).

Ceremonial Stages & Banquet Protocol

Traditional ceremonies followed a structured customary etiquette passed down across generations:

  • First-Day Rituals: Proceedings opened with reverence to the village guardian mother deity (Matama Pujan), the sacred turmeric smearing rite (Halad), and mutual customary gifting between families (Far takane). [1]
  • Musical Accompaniment: As recorded in oral memoirs, wedding processions arrived dancing and singing to the rhythm of indigenous instruments: gachkudi and dafli (Source Line 107).
  • Pangat Etiquette: Dining occurred in parallel seated rows (pangats). Steaming rice, dal, vegetables, hand-rolled wheat polis, freshly fried karanjya, powdered sugar (piti sakhar), and a continuous stream of pure ghee (tupachi dhar) were served. Diners collectively recited Samarth Ramdas's Manache Shlok, commencing the meal after the reverent proclamation 'Sadgurunath Maharaj ki Jai'.

2. Traditional Deities & Religious Observance [edit]

Religious traditions in the Kohli community preserve ancient ties with traditional deities venerated since ancient times in the forested tracts and settlements of eastern Vidarbha (Source Line 31, 37):

Documented Traditional Deities

Community records enumerate the traditional deities (Dev / Parameshwar) with whom the Kohli community holds an ancestral religious relationship (Source Line 37):

  • Kolasur, Tadoba & Kholyakholba: Traditional deities venerated in community tradition, with whom the community holds an ancestral devotional relationship. [1]
  • Kali Devi & Sat Devi: Traditional mother deities with whom the community preserves an ancestral devotional bond.
  • Rama, Shiva, Bhairava & Maruti (Hanuman): Classical deities including Lord Rama, Shiva, Bhairava, and Maruti (Anjaneya / Pawansut Hanuman / Bajrangbali), venerated across community settlements.

Agricultural & Seasonal Festivals

  • Bail Pola: The most prominent agrarian festival, dedicated to honoring working bullocks with decorated horns, embroidered cloths, and village-wide parades reflecting gratitude to cattle.
  • Tulsi Vivah: Ceremonial worship marking the conclusion of the monsoon Chaturmas, celebrated across households and opening the auspicious winter matrimonial calendar.
  • Holi & Rang Panchami: Spring harvest celebration featuring communal bonfires, traditional folk singing, theatrical plays, and vibrant color festivals bringing all village sections together.

3. Village Organization & Social Interdependence [edit]

Because historical Malguzari across eastern Vidarbha was widely held by the Kohli community, Kohli village Patels and agrarian households maintained close, paternal relationships with the entire village social fabric, functioning as recognized patrons of the traditional Bara Balutedar system. [1]

The Patil's Wada & Dahaljit (डहलजीत) Tradition

According to community recollections, village politics and affairs traditionally centered around the Kohli Patil's homestead (*wada*). When the Patil stood in the *dahaljit* (डहलजीत, the outer portico of the house), passing villagers respectfully greeted him with 'Patil Saheb' (Source Line 1).

Bara Balutedar & The Bhatyavar Remuneration System

Rather than monetary transactions, artisanal and service communities received fixed seasonal shares of unhusked paddy (bhatyavar / भात्यावर) after each winter harvest, ensuring equitable mutual subsistence:

  • Artisans Supported: Blacksmiths (khati) and carpenters (sutar) who fabricated and repaired wooden plows and bullock carts, barbers (navhi), and leatherworkers (chambhar).
  • Livestock Care & Farm Coordination: The Patil's household maintained herds of cows and working buffaloes, coordinating with plowing and field assistants across communities (Gond, Gowari, Dhiwar) in a spirit of mutual domestic respect.

4. Folk Arts, Dandaar & Cultural Performances [edit]

Folk performance traditions served both recreation and moral education in Vidarbha's rural settlements. Several family lines within the Kohli community gained regional renown in traditional stage drama and folk performing arts.

  • Dandaar Folk Theatre (दंडार): Performed in customary folk attire across major annual celebrations including Holi, Rang Panchami, Shiv Jayanti, Ram Navami, Pola, Dussehra, and Diwali. [1]
  • Social Awakening & Moral Instruction: Community plays and Dandar performances were consciously staged to deliver social messages, moral education, and cultural awareness to audiences.
  • Drama, Bhajan & Traditional Attire: Community members actively participated in drama (natak), devotional singing (bhajan), and folk theatre (Dandar) in traditional attire across festive occasions (Source Line 41).

5. Food Traditions & Agricultural Produce [edit]

The culinary heritage of the Kohli community evolved alongside tank-fed paddy irrigation and sugarcane cultivation. Staple meals centered on fragrant rice, golden unrefined jaggery, pulses, and country greens.

  • Indigenous Fragrant Rice: Historical cultivation of fragrant varieties such as Luchai, Chinnor, and Dubraj, celebrated across Central India for their distinct aroma and cooking qualities. [3]
  • Sugarcane & Jaggery (Gur): Bullock-operated cane crushers (charak) and boiling pans produced unrefined golden jaggery used in festive sweets and daily cooking.
  • Banquet Delicacies: Feasts featured hand-rolled karanjya, steaming rice with a continuous stream of pure ghee (tupachi dhar), and fine powdered sugar (piti sakhar). [1]

6. Family Life & Community Solidarity [edit]

Prior to 2001, the joint family system (ekatra kutumb paddhati / एकत्र कुटुंब पद्धती) was the foundational cornerstone of social organization within the Kohli community: [1]

  • Multi-Generational Households: Multiple generations, siblings, and extended kin lived jointly under a single expansive roof, with respected elders providing moral guidance and presiding over agricultural planning.
  • Shared Socio-Economic Management: Family members shared agricultural labor, cattle management, and household responsibilities as a united economic collective.
  • Modern Transition & Cohesion: While formal education and urban employment led to the rise of nuclear households after 2001, community unity remains vibrant through kinship gatherings, matrimonial conferences (Vadhu-Var Melawa), and collective festival homecomings.

7. Tradition and Change [edit]

Like every living culture, Kohli traditions have adapted over the past century. Higher education, technical professions in engineering and administration, and migration to urban centers like Nagpur, Pune, and Mumbai have transformed traditional agrarian lifestyles. Yet, ecological stewardship, water conservation awareness, and community cohesion continue to define the modern Kohli ethos.
